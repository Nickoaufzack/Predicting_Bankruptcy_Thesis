\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{ch:literature}

The quantitative prediction of corporate bankruptcy has moved through three
overlapping phases, from classical statistical models over the adoption of
machine learning to a growing concern with class imbalance and model
interpretability. This chapter reviews the strands most relevant to the present
work and isolates the gap the thesis addresses.

Early bankruptcy prediction relied on discriminant analysis and later on logit
models estimated over a handful of financial ratios. These models are transparent
and their coefficients carry an economic sign, but they impose strong assumptions
on the functional form and struggle with the non-linear interactions that
characterize firm-level data. The comprehensive study of \textcite{liang2016} is
representative of the transition to machine learning. It combines financial
ratios with corporate-governance indicators across a large Taiwanese sample and
shows that ensemble classifiers systematically outperform single linear models.
Their work also established much of the feature-category groundwork on which
subsequent studies, including this one, rely. At the same time,
\textcite{liang2016} force the two classes into an even balance, a design choice
that later work has questioned and that the present thesis deliberately avoids.

Because bankruptcies are rare, the treatment of class imbalance has become a
defining methodological question. \textcite{veganzones2018} show that standard
learners deteriorate sharply as the minority share falls, so some form of
rebalancing is necessary. Two families of remedies exist and they differ in what
kind of data the model ends up learning from. Oversampling methods, of which
SMOTE \parencite{chawla2002} is the most widely used, generate additional
synthetic minority observations until the classes are balanced. Undersampling
methods instead reduce the majority class, so every observation the model is
trained on is a firm that actually existed. This difference is the methodological
commitment of the present thesis and Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology} builds directly
on it. The closest existing work is \textcite{wangliu2021}, who study several
undersampling techniques on precisely the dataset used here, combined with
repeated stratified cross-validation and the $F_2$-score as the primary metric.
Their best configuration, edited nearest neighbours with a naive-Bayes
classifier, reaches an $F_2$ of $0.423$. Their sampling philosophy, leakage-aware
validation and choice of metric form the base that this thesis extends.
Cluster-based undersampling in particular has precedent beyond their work, as
\textcite{ansahnarh2024} apply centroid-based reduction on the same data.

A parallel and considerably larger body of recent work reports far higher
performance by combining synthetic oversampling with flexible learners.
\textcite{pham2025}, who also provide a broad overview of sampling strategies,
reach accuracies near $98.6\%$ with SMOTE and an artificial neural network, while
\textcite{suresh2025} and \textcite{sinap2025} report comparable figures with
related pipelines. \textcite{liashenko2023} and \textcite{amirshahi2024} survey
balancing methods and ensembles across both regimes. These results are impressive
at face value, but a substantial part of the minority class these models learn
from, and are sometimes evaluated on, consists of firms that never existed, and
headline accuracy is a misleading summary under a $96.8/3.2$ split. The present
thesis treats this literature as a benchmark to argue against rather than to
emulate, valuing the integrity of the training data over reported performance.

A final strand concerns which features drive the predictions. Several studies
approach this through upfront intervention, either selecting features before
training, as \textcite{chaudhary2023} does with a Pearson-correlation screen, or
transforming them, as \textcite{ko2024} and \textcite{sinap2025} do with
factor analysis and principal components. Such filters are simple, but they
capture only linear association and answer a different question from the one
posed here, ranking features before and independently of the model, whereas this
thesis asks what the trained model actually relies on. Post-hoc importance
methods have been applied in this context, as \textcite{ansahnarh2024} report
random-forest importances and \textcite{sinap2025} employ SHAP, yet these
studies report a single importance measure without examining whether alternative
measures would agree. That is the gap this thesis targets. Rather than adopting
one importance method, it applies native importance, permutation importance and
SHAP side by side and makes their comparison the object of study, on models
trained without any synthetic data. The technical foundations of these methods
are introduced where they are used, in Chapters~\ref{ch:methodology}
and~\ref{ch:featureimportance}.
