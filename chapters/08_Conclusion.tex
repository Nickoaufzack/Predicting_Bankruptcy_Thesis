\chapter{Conclusion}
\label{ch:conclusion}

This thesis set out to predict bankruptcy on the Taiwanese dataset without
synthetic data and to open the resulting models to interpretation. Trained
exclusively on real firms through ClusterCentroids undersampling, evaluated with
repeated stratified cross-validation and selected on the $F_2$-score, gradient
boosting reached $0.550$ and the random forest $0.546$, both clearly ahead of the
logistic regression at $0.482$ and above the best comparable result of
\textcite{wangliu2021} at $0.423$, under a setup that differs in the undersampling
algorithm and the model family, so the numbers indicate rather than prove an
improvement.

The main contribution lies one layer deeper. Where \textcite{wangliu2021} stop at
predictive performance, this thesis adds a feature-importance layer and compares
three methods across three models. The comparison shows that there is no single
answer to which financial ratios matter. Each method departs from the others in
the direction its known weakness predicts, as impurity importance splits credit
between redundant features, permutation hides features with close substitutes and
SHAP shares credit while keeping it readable. A ranking is therefore a joint
product of the data, the model and the method, and any study reporting one list
without this context claims more than the tools can carry. Within these limits the
economic signal is consistent, as every model places a leverage measure at or near
the top, read throughout as association rather than cause.

Two extensions follow naturally. Validating the rankings on a second dataset from
a different market and period would show which parts of the pattern travel beyond
Taiwan. And connecting the surviving features to economic theory in more depth,
ideally with a design that supports causal statements, would turn what this thesis
carefully reports as association into tested explanation.
