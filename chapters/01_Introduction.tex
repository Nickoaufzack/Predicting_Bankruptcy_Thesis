\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

Corporate insolvencies are rising worldwide for the fifth consecutive year, with
\textcite{allianztrade2026} expecting a further increase of $6\%$ in 2026 and
$2.2$ million jobs directly at risk in the failing firms alone. For the firms themselves, creditors,
suppliers, insurers and banks, identifying the risk of failure early is a
fundamental question of risk assessment and it matters even more in times like
these. The most accessible signal for that purpose is what a firm reports about
itself, its financial statements. Ratios describing profitability, liquidity
and debt condense a firm's condition into comparable numbers, which makes them
a natural input for prediction models. Machine learning can pick up patterns in
these ratios that classical methods miss, but it faces one structural problem.
Bankruptcies are rare, and models trained on such imbalanced data tend to
overlook the very cases they are supposed to find. Much of the recent
literature answers this by balancing the dataset through synthetic bankruptcy 
cases and by relying on complex models whose decisions remain largely unexplained.

This thesis takes a different route on both points. Using a widely studied
dataset of $6{,}819$ Taiwanese firms, of which only $3.2\%$ went bankrupt, it
trains exclusively on real observations. The imbalance is handled by
ClusterCentroids undersampling, which condenses the healthy majority into
representative real firms instead of generating synthetic bankrupt ones. Three
models of increasing complexity, a logistic regression, a random forest and
gradient boosting, are compared under repeated stratified cross-validation and
selected on the $F_2$-score, because missing a bankruptcy costs far more than a
false alarm. The setup extends \textcite{wangliu2021}, who established this
sampling and evaluation framework on the same data but stopped at predictive
performance. The goal is to build an approach that is based on but not necessarily 
tied to this particular dataset and could therefore be transferred to any similarly 
imbalanced prediction problem.

An additional contribution builds on top of this framework and asks a second
question, which financial features the fitted models rely on and how far the
answer depends on how one asks. Three feature importance methods, native importance, 
permutation importance and SHAP, are applied to all three models. The short 
answer is that no single ranking exists, as the methods
diverge in ways their known weaknesses predict, while a consistent signal
remains, with every model placing a measure of debt dependence at or near the
top. The insight therefore lies not in one definitive ranking but in the comparison
itself, which shows how each answer is shaped by the model and the method
behind it. Chapters~2 and~3 review the literature and the data, Chapters~4 and~5
develop the methodology and the importance methods and Chapters~6 to~8 report,
discuss and conclude.
